Two scores, two questions

The question behind this map is where in the Chicago area you can get the best mix of affordability, neighborhood quality and day-to-day practicality. That is really two questions, so the page answers them separately.

  • Fit to you (your preferences): how close a zip comes to the targets you set. It changes with every target and weight, and it says nothing about whether a place is cheap for what it is.
  • Price vs expected (a fixed model): whether a zip sells for less or more per square foot than zips with the same measured schools, safety, commute, walkability, train access, home age and state. It is the same for everyone and ignores your targets.

A desirable zip isn't automatically good value, and a zip that's cheap for its features isn't automatically desirable. The chart above puts the two together.

How the fit score works

  • Give each factor a target and a weight. The weights are your choice; everything else below is a fixed rule.
  • Each value is turned into a percentile among the area's zips, so minutes, dollars and crime rates can be compared. A factor's match is 100 minus the gap, in percentile points, between the zip and your target. Beating a target for schools, safety, price or train station counts as a match of 100; for commute, walkability and home age being off either way costs points.
  • The fit score is the weighted average of the matches, 0 to 100. The detail panel shows each factor's share of your weight and the points it costs, which add up to 100 minus the score.
  • Missing data is left out, not guessed: the score uses the factors the zip has data for, and the panel and table say what's missing. A zip is shown but not ranked when the missing factors carry more than 20% of your weight. (Until Oct 22, 2026, a missing value counted as an average one, which flattered zips whose police didn't report.)
  • With two people, “best for both” uses the lower of the two scores.
  • Walkability and distance to a train station go together closely (ρ = −0.82 across zips), as do safety and schools to a lesser degree. Weighting both of a related pair counts much the same thing twice; the page points this out when it happens.

How “price vs expected” works

A regression of log price per square foot on the six measured factors, plus terms for Indiana (different taxes and tests) and the city of Chicago (one school district): log($/sq ft) = b0 + b1·schools + b2·log(1 + crime) + b3·commute + b4·walkability + b5·log(miles to station + 0.25) + b6·home age + b7·Indiana + b8·Chicago. It explains 0.67 of the variation across 338 zips. A zip's expected price comes from the model fitted without that zip, so its own price can't pull the estimate towards itself. Prices within ±10% of expected count as in line; 86 zips are below that and 99 above.

Confidence is high, medium or low depending on how many of these doubts apply: fewer than 50 sales (a zip's median price per sq ft is typically off by about 6% at 50 sales and 3% at 200, estimated from how much it moves between years), a missing factor, a Chicago zip (the model can't see school differences inside the city), a different class in most of 12 other model versions, a different class with last year's prices, or a gap that price noise alone could erase. Currently 159 high, 107 medium and 72 low.

What it does not mean. “Below expected” is not “undervalued” and not a forecast. The gap holds whatever the model can't see: lot size, condition, taxes, flood risk, a lake view, school quality inside Chicago, or a town's finances. The zips furthest below expected are largely places with long-running disinvestment, such as Gary, Harvey and parts of Chicago's South and West Sides, which is exactly what the measured factors miss. The gaps are also very stable: they correlate at 0.97 with the same model on last year's prices (0.94 with 2023's), so they are lasting differences between places, not short-term mispricing waiting to close.

Is the model any good?

Each model below was fitted on nine tenths of the zips and tested on the rest, ten times. With spatial folds the held-out zips are clusters of neighbors, so a zip is never predicted from the zip next door; that's the honest test, because nearby zips share things no model measures. The error is the typical (median) miss in price per sq ft.

The pre-registered model and its successor barely beat a flat average once neighbors are held out; letting each factor carry its own weight, on a log scale, cuts the spatial error by about a fifth and explains about half the variation in unseen areas. Adding the housing-stock mix (share of single-family homes, rooms) didn't help out of sample, so it's only a sensitivity check. The residuals are still spatially clustered (Moran's I = 0.26, down from 0.59 for the pre-registered model), so the p-values below are optimistic; the county-clustered column is the more cautious one.

The data

  • Schools: district test scores, as a percentile within the state (Illinois Report Card, Indiana DOE). Chicago is one district, so every city zip gets the same value.
  • Safety: crimes per 1,000 residents a year: violent plus property offenses reported to the FBI by the zip's police agency. Inside Chicago, the city's FBI total is spread across zips by where the city's own incident data puts the same offenses, so city neighborhoods are no longer all the same. Offenses are counted where they happen, so downtown zips with few residents get very high rates.
  • Commute: the Census average, or the drive time to where you work (OpenStreetMap, no traffic).
  • Train station: straight-line miles to the nearest Metra, CTA 'L' or South Shore Line station, averaged over where people in the zip live (Census 2020 population centers).
  • Walkability: EPA index, 1 to 20.
  • Home age and rent: Census ACS 2020–2024. Rent is per person: the median rent divided by the average bedrooms of rented homes, so a $2,500 two-bedroom is $1,250 each.
  • Price: median sale price and price per sq ft, June 2025 to May 2026, all home types together (Redfin). Zips need at least 20 sales.

Limits

  • A zip averages very different streets, so treat a result as a place worth a visit, not a verdict.
  • The measured factors leave out a lot that people pay for: lot size, condition, taxes and fees, flood and noise risk, parks and the lake, and school quality within Chicago.
  • Crime counts depend on what each agency reports; 38 zips have no usable figure, and small towns' rates are noisy.
  • Prices are one year of medians, not individual sales, and mix condos and houses. There's no trend or forecast here: nothing on this page predicts appreciation.
  • Renters only get the fit score: ACS rents are five-year averages that include long-time tenants, too blunt for a “rent vs expected” estimate.
  • Race and ethnicity, and proxies for them, aren't used anywhere.

The pre-registered test (a simpler price model, decided before prices were downloaded) and every later change, with dates and reasons, are in the repo on GitHub, along with the code behind every number here.
