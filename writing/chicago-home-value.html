Is there a cheap good zip code in Chicagoland?

I scored 339 Chicago-area zip codes on schools, safety, commute and walkability, and looked for the ones that sell for less than their quality predicts. The answer is yes, but only just.

Data and code ← All writing

Ask anyone where to buy a house around Chicago and you'll hear the same names: Naperville, Hinsdale, Evanston, the North Shore. Those places are good, and you pay for it. I wanted to know whether there are places that are nearly as good but cost a lot less, and whether they're places nobody mentions.

The question, set in advance

It's easy to fool yourself with data like this: run the numbers, see something interesting, then decide that's what you were looking for. To avoid that, I wrote down the hypothesis, every definition and the pass/fail rule in one file and committed it before downloading any price data. Scientists call this pre-registration. Anything I changed later is logged in that file with a reason.

Among zip codes in the Chicago metro, some above-median-quality zips sell for much less per square foot than their quality and property taxes predict, and most of them are not on the usual "best places to live" list.

The rule: the hypothesis holds if at least 3 zips are both high quality and significantly underpriced, and more than half of those aren't on the reputation list. Otherwise it fails. Either answer would get this post.

How it works

The area. The 13 counties of the Chicago metro, 9 in Illinois and 4 in Northwest Indiana. Zips with fewer than 20 sales in the past year were dropped, which left 339.

A quality score from 0 to 100. Four things a homebuyer cares about, each turned into a percentile rank and averaged with equal weight:

  • Schools: district test proficiency, ranked within each state first, since Illinois and Indiana use different tests.
  • Safety: crimes per 1,000 residents from the FBI's data, for the police department covering the zip.
  • Commute: average time to work, from the Census.
  • Walkability: the EPA's National Walkability Index.

Nothing price-based goes into the score, since that would make the test circular. Race and ethnicity, and stand-ins for them, aren't used anywhere. Steering buyers by the makeup of a neighborhood is what fair-housing law forbids and what redlining did.

A model of price. A linear regression predicts each zip's median price per square foot from its quality score and its property tax rate. The residual is the actual price minus the predicted one. A negative residual means the zip sells for less than the model expects.

A value pick is a zip with above-median quality whose residual is more than one standard deviation below zero, which works out to at least $63 per square foot cheaper than predicted.

The reputation list is Niche's 2026 "Best Places to Live in Illinois" top 20, plus the Chicago-area places in the top 50 of its Indiana ranking, matched to zip codes.

The chart

Each dot is a zip. Further right means higher quality. Lower means cheaper than the model predicts. Value picks sit in the shaded corner. Hover or tap a dot for details.

The result: supported, by the rule

The main model finds 16 value picks, and 11 of them aren't on the reputation list. That passes the rule I set beforehand, so the hypothesis is supported.

The reputation list is also roughly where you'd expect. Its 32 zips have a median quality of 62, against 49 for everywhere else, and they typically sell about $27 per square foot above what the model predicts. Some of that premium is the name.

Why I only half believe it

A pass by the rule isn't the end of it. I'd also decided in advance to stress-test the result, and it doesn't hold up as well as the headline suggests.

  • Indiana does a lot of the work. 9 of the 16 picks are in Indiana, and Indiana zips as a group sit about $61 per square foot below the line. I put the tax rate in the model to account for Indiana's cap on homestead property taxes, but the model gave it almost no weight. So part of "underpriced" here just means "in Indiana". All 5 picks that are on the Niche list are Indiana towns (Valparaiso twice, Munster, Chesterton and Dyer).
  • Illinois on its own still passes. Rerunning everything on Illinois zips alone gives 11 picks, none of them on the list. This is the strongest evidence for the hypothesis.
  • The picks depend on the recipe. When I rebuilt the score four times, leaving out one ingredient each time, only 4 of the 16 picks stayed picks in at least three of the four versions.
  • Some picks are data artifacts. I looked at the top 10 by hand. Robbins scores well partly because its police department reports no crime data, so its score is the average of the other three. Small rural towns like Kentland and Rensselaer get great commute scores because people work locally, not because they're close to Chicago jobs. North Chicago's short commutes come from Naval Station Great Lakes.
  • The model only explains a quarter of prices (R² = 0.25). Lot size, the lake, home condition, flood risk and airport noise all matter and aren't in it.

Follow-up: what a house costs to run

After seeing the results, I wanted to know whether running costs explain some of the gap. A cheaper house with high energy bills or a long drive isn't much of a deal. So I added a second model with three more inputs: median home age, the typical monthly electricity and gas bill (both from the Census), and the yearly cost of gasoline for the commute.

Since I added this after seeing the results, it doesn't change the verdict above. It's labeled as a follow-up in the change log. Switch the chart to "Follow-up model" to see it.

  • It explains more: R² rises from 0.25 to 0.41.
  • Energy bills and commuting fuel both pull prices down, as you'd expect. Home age makes no measurable difference once the others are in.
  • It still finds 16 picks, now with 15 off the reputation list and only 4 in Indiana. Cheaper gas and energy in Indiana explain part of its lower prices.

Take the cost numbers loosely. Energy bills grow with house size, and the fuel estimate is built from commute time, which the quality score already includes.

So where would I look?

Woodstock, Grayslake and Itasca are suburban picks with complete data, and each shows up in at least two versions of the analysis (main, Illinois-only, follow-up). Woodstock shows up in all three. Joliet's east side (60433) is one of the four picks that survive the leave-one-out test. These are leads worth a weekend drive, not proof of a bargain. A zip code averages over very different streets, and a model this simple can't see what makes a particular block cheap.

What I'd tell someone doing this again

  • Write the rule down first. Without it, I'd have been tempted to drop the Indiana caveat, or to make the follow-up model the headline because it looks better.
  • Missing data quietly flatters. A town with no crime data looks safer than it is unless you flag it.
  • Run the checks you set up in advance even after the headline passes. They're what made this post honest.
