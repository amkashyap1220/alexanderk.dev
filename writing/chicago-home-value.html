How the score works

You give each of six things a target (the value you want) and a weight (how much it matters):

  • Schools: district test proficiency as a percentile, ranked within each state, since Illinois and Indiana use different tests (Illinois Report Card, Indiana DOE).
  • Safety: crimes per 1,000 residents a year for the police department covering the zip (FBI Crime Data Explorer).
  • Commute: average one-way time to work (Census ACS).
  • Walkability: the EPA's National Walkability Index, 1 to 20.
  • Home age: how old the median home is (Census ACS).
  • Price: median sale price over the past year (Redfin), or median monthly rent (Census ACS).

To compare minutes with dollars with crime rates, every value is turned into a percentile: where it falls among the metro's zips, from 0 to 100. A zip's closeness to a target is 100 minus the gap between their percentiles. It's 100 when the zip is exactly on target, and lower the further off it is. For schools, safety and price, beating your target (better schools, less crime, a lower price) also counts as 100. For commute, walkability and home age, being off in either direction lowers it, since you may want a longer commute for more space, or an older house. The score is the weighted average of the six:

score = Σ (weight × closeness) ÷ Σ weight

So if price is the only thing at Top and the rest are Low, a zip whose median price is exactly your target scores close to 100. Your top 20 are the 20 highest scores. With a second person, each of you gets a score, and "best for both" ranks zips by the lower of the two, so neither of you ends up somewhere far from what you wanted.

The area is the 13 counties of the Chicago metro, 9 in Illinois and 4 in Northwest Indiana. For buying, a zip needs at least 20 sales in the past year (338 zips); for renting, at least 100 rented homes (347). A missing number, usually crime data a police department didn't report, counts as 50. Race and ethnicity, and stand-ins for them, aren't used anywhere: steering people by the makeup of a neighborhood is what fair-housing law forbids and what redlining did.

How your picks compare with Niche

The diamonds on the map are the places people usually recommend: Niche's 2026 "Best Places to Live in Illinois" top 20, plus the Chicago-area places in the top 50 of its Indiana ranking, matched to their zip codes. They stay on the map whatever you set, so you can see how your targets line up with them, and the line above the map counts how many of your top 20 are among them.

What to keep in mind

  • A zip code averages over very different streets. Treat a pick as a place worth a weekend drive, not proof of a bargain.
  • Percentiles only compare zips with each other, and lot size, flood risk, airport noise and home condition aren't in the data.
