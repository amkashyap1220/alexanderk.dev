Where's the best home value in Chicagoland?

I scored 338 Chicago-area zip codes on quality and on what a home costs to run, ranked the best values, then checked them against the places everyone recommends.

Data and code ← All writing

Ask anyone where to buy a house around Chicago and you'll hear the same names: Naperville, Hinsdale, Evanston, the North Shore. I wanted to find the best value instead: places where you get the most for your money once you count what a home costs to own and run, not only its sale price. Then I wanted to check whether any of them are places nobody mentions.

How I scored value

The area. The 13 counties of the Chicago metro, 9 in Illinois and 4 in Northwest Indiana. Zips with fewer than 20 sales in the past year were dropped, which left 338.

A quality score from 0 to 100. Four things a homebuyer cares about, each turned into a percentile rank and averaged with equal weight:

  • Schools: district test proficiency, ranked within each state first, since Illinois and Indiana use different tests.
  • Safety: crimes per 1,000 residents from the FBI's data, for the police department covering the zip.
  • Commute: average time to work, from the Census.
  • Walkability: the EPA's National Walkability Index.

Nothing price-based goes into the score, since that would make the test circular. Race and ethnicity, and stand-ins for them, aren't used anywhere. Steering buyers by the makeup of a neighborhood is what fair-housing law forbids and what redlining did.

What a home costs to own and run. Next to quality, the model accounts for four costs that come on top of the price:

  • Property tax rate: taxes paid as a share of home value, from the Census.
  • Home energy: the typical monthly electricity and gas bill, from the Census.
  • Commuting fuel: a yearly gasoline estimate from how many people drive, how long they drive, and the regional pump price (EIA).
  • Home age: how old the typical house is, from the Census.

The model. A linear regression predicts each zip's median price per square foot from its quality score and those four costs. The residual is the actual price minus the predicted one. A negative residual means homes there sell for less than the model expects for what you get.

A value pick is a zip with above-median quality whose residual is more than one standard deviation below zero. That works out to at least $56 per square foot below its predicted price.

12 are in Illinois and 4 in Indiana. Most are just above the quality median (scores in the 50s and 60s), not top-tier areas. That's what the model is built to find: solid places at a clear discount. Quality has the clearest effect, adding about $1.90 per square foot for every point. Energy bills and commuting fuel both pull prices down, as you'd expect. Once those are in, home age and the tax rate make no measurable difference.

How much to trust the list

Before looking at prices, I decided how I'd stress-test the result. Some picks hold up and some don't.

  • Change the recipe. I rebuilt the score four times, leaving out one ingredient each time. 7 of the 16 picks stayed picks in at least three of the four versions: Kentland, North Chicago, Joliet's east side (60433), Rensselaer, Woodstock, Valparaiso (46383) and Schaumburg (60173).
  • Illinois on its own. Rerunning everything on Illinois zips alone gives 15 picks. Woodstock, Joliet 60433, Itasca and Sycamore are among them.
  • Some picks are data artifacts. I checked the most underpriced zips by hand. Robbins scores well partly because its police department reports no crime data, so its score is the average of the other three ingredients. Small rural towns like Kentland and Rensselaer get great commute scores because people work locally, not because they're close to Chicago jobs. North Chicago's short commutes come from Naval Station Great Lakes.
  • The model explains 41% of prices (R² = 0.41). Lot size, the lake, home condition, flood risk and airport noise all matter and aren't in it.

The strongest leads are suburban, have complete data and hold up under the checks: Woodstock and Joliet's east side (60433) pass both, Schaumburg (60173) survives the recipe changes, and Itasca and Sycamore hold in the Illinois-only run. They're leads worth a weekend drive, not proof of a bargain. A zip code averages over very different streets, and a model this simple can't see what makes a particular block a good value.

Did I find hidden value?

Now for the comparison with word of mouth. For the reputation list I used Niche's 2026 "Best Places to Live in Illinois" top 20, plus the Chicago-area places in the top 50 of its Indiana ranking, matched to 32 zip codes.

15 of my 16 value picks are not on the list. The only overlap is Valparaiso (46383).

Before seeing any prices, I wrote down the rule for calling this a yes: at least 3 value picks, with more than half of them off the reputation list. The result clears it easily. The list's own zips are well chosen but expensive. Their median quality is 62, against 49 everywhere else. They typically sell about $27 per square foot above what the model predicts, and 62% of them sit above the line. Part of what you pay for in those places is the name.

Notes on the method

  • I set the method before looking. It's easy to fool yourself with data like this: run the numbers, see something interesting, then decide that's what you were looking for. So I wrote down the question, every definition and the pass/fail rule, and committed them before downloading any price data. Scientists call this pre-registration.
  • One change came after the first run. The pre-registered model only had quality and the tax rate. After running it, I added energy bills, commuting fuel and home age, because a buyer pays those costs on top of the price. Changing a model after seeing results is exactly what pre-registration exists to catch, so it's logged with the reason in the decision file. The first run also cleared the rule (16 picks, 11 off the list), but 9 of its picks were in Indiana. Lower gas and energy costs in Indiana explain part of that, and adding them cut Indiana's picks to 4.
  • The cost numbers are rough. Energy bills grow with house size, and the fuel estimate is built from commute time, which the quality score already includes.
